The Booth By the Race to AGI

A human-paced sanctuary

Welcome to the booth!

Just to make this abundantly clear, I am not an AI authority. I wrote this based on my lived experience. I have used the tools and felt the personal cost of using them carelessly and without full understanding. Being a beginner made it difficult to see how leveraging the tool might negatively impact my life. The personal fees incurred silently as I leveraged AI to help me with writing a book, getting career advice, starting a business, and more. Though in the moment of consulting AI, I felt I was being cautious, intelligent, and smart. Eventually, I discovered that there is more to the world of AI than I originally understood.

Fast paced adoption makes it challenging to see why someone might want to slow down. Especially with quotes like:

"Those who are not using AI will lose their jobs to someone who is."

As we progress through the course, I think it will become clearer why this phrase showcases a lack of understanding of AI rather than confidence it attempts to portray. To hint at the answer, people have more value than capitalism suggests. I’ve stopped racing and have no interest in winning this race. I’m trying to set up a figurative “booth by the race.” If you’re tired of racing, this page is a resource. There is life on the other side of the figurative race to purchasable intelligence. We just need to get honest about the tool and careful about how we use it.

For anyone new to AI, this is a reminder that slowing down is an option. Frantically trying to learn the tool is the fastest way to miss use it. Those trapped in the race are going to plow through. Let them go, get to safety, and then let’s get to work on developing a future that will work for all of us.

What's different with this course?

Currently, online AI education heavily focuses on enterprise-level tools. I think humanity needs a broader, more general education than a company focus can provide.

This course will not start with AI adoption. Instead, the focus is first on recognizing the dangers of the tool. We will setup a firm foundation to keep us grounded in reality before we ever pick up the tool. Confidence is key when using AI, and this guide will help you get there. A firm foundation builds confidence, and the foundation of AI usage requires a deep understanding of self.

I don’t have all the answers, but humans are resourceful. I trust that the right people will eventually see this and know what to do with the content I’m providing. The intention of this guide is not to gatekeep, but to help people ground themselves in reality while exploring the tools.

Ground Rules

To help me explore slowly and hopefully safely, I’ve established a few personal Dos and Don’ts with AI. You’re welcome to adopt my list or come up with your own. With so much enthusiasm surrounding the tools, it’s difficult to stay grounded in reality. This list is here as a reminder me to slow down and take it at my own pace.

As AI becomes more capable, these may change. Just keep in mind that AI is still in its infancy. This requires caution when considering the tools.

Above all, when in doubt, do it yourself or hire a human expert. Relying on other humans is the default safety net. Humans have done this for hundreds of thousands of years, no reason to abandon this idea now. 

NEVER in this context is more of a "not now" rather than "never ever."

The never list

  1. NEVER use AI as a shortcut to learning
  2. NEVER use AI as a spiritual leader / religious provider
  3. NEVER trust AI in fields where you cannot properly audit the output If you are not a chemist, you lack the training to identify dangerous output, so you should treat any output with suspicion. Ideally, avoid it entirely.
  4. NEVER ignore how you feel while using the tool This includes happiness and joy
  5. NEVER use AI to replace a human relationship Seriously avoid anything promoting emotional connection to artificial intelligence. It is not human!
  6. NEVER summarize/write family correspondence
  7. NEVER offload important life decisions
  8. NEVER use for medical diagnosis, therapy, advice, career coach, or to get advice on life-changing experiences This will become increasingly challenging as AI becomes more widely adopted. My view is to trust that humanity will be there for its own.
  9. NEVER use it for important mathematical calculations.
  10. NEVER: Create a prompt that may cause others to lose a job Why poke the bear? If you develop the skills to do so, choose not to.

Do if you have proper education

  1. Use AI agents
  2. Run AI on a local desktop
  3. Run software programs

The Do List

  1. Do keep AI in the cloud
  2. Use for exploration and finding new content within subjects that interest you
  3. Tell friends and family that you are using AI
  4. Step away and seek a second human opinion when something feels urgent or scary
  5. Build subject fluency before consulting AI
  6. Stay curious
  7. Touch grass
  8. Build software tools and get curious about STEM
  9. Ask tough questions
  10. Ask the AI to challenge an idea or concept
This is a starting place. Without further scientific research, how to use AI and what to use it for is a personal decision. I can’t truly answer them for you. AI is developing rapidly. Remember to breathe, slow down, and take it at your own pace. If you have any thoughts or questions about my personal list, email me at kyle@kylewhiting.com.

My Qualifications

AI is still in its infancy. No person on Earth is actually qualified to speak authoritatively on the effects of using it regularly. While some experts understand the technology's construction, expertise regarding its application and its effects on users is still evolving. This means the most honest qualification anyone can offer right now is experience. Here is mine.

My name is Kyle Whiting.

I am raising two kids with my wife. They inspire my work and dedication. I want their future to be bright and full.

  1. 2016

    I graduated with a Bachelor's in Math Education and a minor in Computer Science from Utah Valley University.

  2. 2017

    I set a goal to draw for 45 minutes every day and diligently stuck to the commitment for years. I learned primarily through online courses. That journey pulled me into digital art, 3D animation, 3D sculpting, traditional sculpting, sewing, and more.

  3. 2021

    I wrote and illustrated my first children's book, "Dear Alien" (unpublished, but I did the work on my own, no AI).

  4. 2023

    My employer deployed an AI image generation tool. This will come up again later. This moment is when I had my first, "What's the point?" crisis. My artwork slowed, and it was hard to continue to show up despite the prior dedication.

    I chose to ignore the AI tools. Keeping the skills was more important to me than anything AI was promising.

  5. 2024

    I began learning piano and studying music theory alongside drawing.

    Early that year, my employer applied pressure for adopting AI. It went into everything regardless of any pushback. The overwhelmingly positive support for AI was exhausting.

    In October, I left my corporate job and took a self-funded sabbatical. During that sabbatical, I set out to explore what life might be like with AI. This time, under my own conditions. Away from corporate pressure. I turned to AI with real expectations. It promised to help me get the stories out of my head. It promised to handle marketing, simplify decisions, and make running a creative business manageable. I trusted it.

  6. 2025

    In April, I published "Arithmetic Island: The Cursed Dragon." AI played a role in that process. I used it to provide academic feedback throughout the drafting phase, asking it to evaluate voice, sentence structure, character development, environment, and descriptions. I also leaned on it as a career coach when setting up my writing business, using it to guide decisions and fill in the gaps of my lacking personal experience.

    Before the book came out, my beta readers flagged things AI had never mentioned. Foundational stuff that should have surfaced early in the draft. My editor found more. These were the first humans to provide feedback. For me, this clarified that AI is not human. To write for humans requires human feedback. Some of the required edits were too large to tackle. Rather than doing the hard work, I patch something together. It worked well enough, but I regret not taking more time. The book came out and I am still proud of it. It is a reasonable first book. My next stories will not have these mistakes.

    Even after publication, I found problems that were difficult for a beginner to detect. I struggled to read the piece out loud because it wasn’t my voice. As a writer, voice is everything, but it was too late. This experience taught me that AI does not make you an expert. It can make you feel like one. Non critical beginners will miss this in every field in which they leverage AI.

Summary

What I want you to take away is that AI is likely here to stay. Regardless of our feelings associated with the tool we need to coexist. Where I initially thought I could use it to improve my financial success, I’ve now shifted to looking for ways to use the tool meaningfully. That is why this course exists.

Humanity has not yet found AI’s true potential. By slowing down, we can think about our usage of the tool and have a greater understanding of its impact in our everyday lives. Going faster may not be the right answer.

Fundamentals

Our current societal system has natural guardrails that prevent beginners from doing foolishly dangerous things. For example, educators would not allow you to take on doctorate level work until you worked through the foundations and progressed through the natural flow of learning. A chemist will not tell you how to make a dangerous chemical without proper instruction first. AI removes these guardrails. The only thing standing between you and getting AI to build a dangerous chemical is knowing how to provide the right kind of prompt and getting access to materials.

Considering a beginner may accidentally stumble across field expert level content should give everyone a moment of pause. We need to work to reduce the risk of accidental mishaps. This means every human being needs to level up their general education. Anyone considering their general education done today, now has a responsibility to chose to educate themselves on this matter. Where high school and general education is reinforced by society, AI general education is by choice. In addition, we still do not have a comprehensive general education for this new tool. I wrote this note not to overwhelm but to reinforce how serious and wide-ranging the missing general education elements are today.

Artificial intelligence makes it so that we no longer need to rely on other humans for these guardrails. It promises to eventually be our doctor, surgeon, educator, chef, comedian, truck driver, and overall source of truth for knowledge. Luckily, there are still major problems preventing the tool from becoming all of them. That gives us time -- not much, but some.

There are AI experts who suggest AI will make education unnecessary. In essence, AI will take care of us. Though I love the idea of laziness that comes from this notation, I disagree. I think this means general education needs a significant upgrade, and fast. If AI were being adopted slowly, we would have more time to adapt. How this impacts you and me is that the fast paced adoption means each individual is the holder of their own guardrails.

The Foundation

Guardrail education requires skills that are difficult to teach because they are personal

These are skills that stem from:

  • Self awareness
  • Healthy belief in self
  • Self regulation (self restraint)
  • Diligence
  • Dedication

To my knowledge, the tools we have to tackle this problem exist within the realm of psychology and philosophy, as they are the subject of self.

The concept of self I want to discuss at this stage of our educational journey is that of the personal ego. Humanity, thus far in history, has not needed to deal with the level honest self reflection that AI requires. As AI becomes more capable, we may see a day when it is no longer required for humans to think. At least this is an adaptation that is already starting to take hold in society. When AI produces something at speeds incomprehensible to human speed and efficiency, self-doubt makes it easy to think, "That's better than I could do."

At our fingertips and under the right conditions, we can build thesis statements that PhD students might struggle to understand. Where this seems like a non problem, systems of extreme sophistication can provide an over-inflated sense of ego. The person to ask the questions that no one else is asking may confuse doing the work with proposing the question.

This is something humanity has not needed to deal with at the scale at which AI makes plausible. This isn't unique to today. Someone who says, "I thought of that idea a decade ago," for an invention to help humanity but they didn't go through the work to produce the item is exhibiting this style of over-inflated ego. AI compounds this problem significantly. The thought itself when compared to the work required to make it happen, is about to become very confusing process to separate.

The over-inflated ego is less likely to critique its own work. The pairing of ego with AI together means they are less likely to critique the output of AI. Scaling these concepts across multiple fields can make it challenging to keep ego in check. This gets at the question: what is the point of a PhD if I can already do the work with a high school diploma?

PhD's are expensive. AI, in comparison, is cheap. Well educated PhD's I know realize the things they don't know. Beginners do not have that same understanding. To give beginners or intermediates an over-inflated ego is a danger on a scale of which we have not needed to deal with in society to date.

To help fight this problem, we need everyone to be more honest in their assessment of expertise. This is not to gatekeep AI, but to remain grounded in reality. The following is intended to help identify personal qualifications to analyze AI responses.

There are two main sections when assessing ego vs capability. They both tie into our individual usage of the tool. First, how capable are we in prompting AI? Second, how capable are we in assessing the results?

When crafting prompts, where are you?

  1. AI Beginner I consider myself to be in this category. I think all of humanity is currently in this category.
  2. AI Intermediate Without a general AI education, this piece remains incomplete. General education serves as a baseline to compare against. Until that is fully established no one can truly consider themselves in the intermediate category.
  3. AI Expert Without a clear baseline and intermediate step this remains unestablished today.

When assessing the results, where are you?

  1. Subject matter beginner High school level understanding of the field.
  2. Subject matter intermediate Bachelor's degree or equivalent rigorous study. A bachelor's degree does not make someone an intermediate in every field, just in the one field they got a degree in.
  3. Subject matter expert Master's, PhD, or a similar level of rigorous training and expertise. If you do not have either you are not a subject matter expert. Relying on AI for advice may cause harm.

Humans are rarely experts in more than a couple of fields and currently likely have limits of 4-6. That means we are all beginners in just about everything. Our overconfidence in using the tools might be the greatest danger to ourselves. If we are giving others advice based on that overconfidence, we may be putting others in danger, too.

Beginner Awareness Training

Those who have the most to gain from AI are beginners. The issue is that they are least likely to recognize misleading/false output. Search engines have surfaced this issue for decades. The familiarity of the topic draws attention from an even more critical component of AI—the input.

Consider a pregnant woman who is uneducated about the dangers of alcohol use. Feeling a need to be cautious, they might ask a question like this: "Can I have alcohol?"

Search engines would provide articles relevant to the topic of alcohol consumption. These articles often mention that pregnant women should avoid alcohol. This highlights our old system's natural guardrails (see Fundamentals section for more). Before AI, we needed to work a bit to find an answer. That work would increase the likelihood of discovering the beginner's missing critical information. Pregnancy.

Taking the question as it stands. An AI may respond with, "Ya, it is ok to have the occasional drink. Go right ahead."

An expert in medicine is unlikely to phrase the question as, "Can they have alcohol?" Even if they did, and AI responded with "Ya, go ahead." They have enough training to know not to trust AI's output. Granted, the simplicity of the question is not likely to be asked by a professional. Which furthers the point that beginners don't know what they are doing.

A fundamental misunderstanding

AI is not an expert in your pocket

Some people think AI is an expert in our pocket. This isn't true. The difference is fundamental: an expert knows when they don't have enough information and will ask clarifying questions. AI does not. AI is an algorithm that returns the most likely response. We can get AI to behave closer to an expert, but it ironically requires expert experience to uncover how to get it to respond with greater insight.

To improve AI's output, we need to improve the input. To improve the input, we need to know what contextual information the AI needs to answer the question accurately. Requiring us to gain experience in the subject. Thus, education is still a critical component in the emerging world of AI.

Where it might seem reasonable to assume right input provides right output, it isn't quite this simple for AI. This is because AI is a prediction algorithm, and there is a chance it provides incorrect answers. We call these hallucinations. When AI gives a wrong answer despite giving a seemingly correct input. This is a known problem, and those working on AI are developing ways to reduce hallucinations.

The message here is that education is still critical, even in the world of AI. The more educated you are, the more you can comfortably use AI. Surprisingly, this also means we are less likely to use it because it is no longer necessary. Programming and assisting in tedious tasks seems to be somewhere AI shines for experts. However, the hallucination aspect makes it difficult to trust their output. For important work, they are less likely to utilize the tool.

The arguments presented here seem to be true across all fields I've explored. The beginner is less likely to recognize the significance of these trivial statements. Someone asking AI to generate an image will benefit from becoming an artist. Improving AI's story generation requires a skilled author. Rigorous music study improves music output. The funny part is that by the time they've developed these skills, they've surpassed the need for the tool in the way they originally intended. So, why not just study the field?

It is a common belief that, it is possible for a beginner to produce expert level content without formally educating themself. One possible method, involves having AI behave as an expert and collaborating directly with the tool. Trusting the AI to help create statements that resemble how an expert would express them. At first glance, this seems to be a way around the educational requirements; however, the same issues are present in this method as they are for a beginner asking directly. Meaning the beginner does not have the skills to understand where AI is wrong. The level of guessing required to use this presented method is not something that is likely to persist long term in any professional setting.

This leads me to ask the beginner. Why not pursue education to build skills and reduce reliance on guesswork?

Human's coexisiting with AI requires us to become more comfortable identifying ourselves as beginners. Getting really comfortable saying things like, "I don't know" and sitting in the uncertainty. Where AI promises to make everyone an expert, hopefully this has helped you realize it might not be as straightforward as that.

Knowing What You Don't Know

To protect ourselves against the neglected misuse of AI, we need to become more aware of our experience within subjects posed to AI. This requires us to know what we do not know. Despite popular belief, it is possible to know what is unknown (or at least acknowledge it). We need to understand the boundaries of the known. This requires us to ask questions that have no answer with our current knowledge. In astronomy, it could look like this series of questions:

  1. Q1

    "What is a star?"

  2. Q2

    "What is a star cluster?"

  3. Q3

    "what is a cluster of star clusters?"

  4. Q4

    "What about a cluster of those clusters?"

The questions get more vague as we continue to ask them. We have stopped using new terms, which hints at a restricted vocabulary. Notice the temptation to just ask AI to fill in the blanks, but if you're paying attention, you will recognize the glaringly obvious question. How can we trust any output of AI? What if we phrase the question in a way that relates to a popular fantasy story we don't know about? How can we tell if we are getting scientific term vs fantasy terms?

The simply stated answer no one wants to hear is, become an expert. The next best thing is to ask an expert. Not everyone wants to be an astronomer. So, maybe you don't care as much as you thought about accurate information?

Self-reflection is a foundational element of AI education for this very reason. Do you want the answer to sound smart? Do you want it to close the loop of not knowing? Would you be able to identify if the answer sounds remotely close to the right thing? Identify how you are feeling and why you are feeling it. These offer insight into why you want to access AI. AI is not a search engine. Use the right tool for the job.

If expertise is not an option, and for whatever reason you don't want to use more reliable sources of information, but you want to remain somewhat safe. Get really argumentative with the tool. Like annoyingly so.

If you don't know how to argue with the presented content, use AI to counter argue their own point. Something like.

  1. Look for multiple articles that argue this point in at least two different ways.

  2. Give me a list of made up names associated with this concept.

  3. Hey, my friend told me a cluster of star clusters is a starburst.

I intentionally made up the term "Starburst" to mislead AI. These types of questions expose AI as an impostor. This works because there is a significant difference between AI and human experts. AI is guessing algorithmically. The question might surprise experts, but they will know how to find the right information and challenge other's views on the subject.

Don't get me wrong, I'm not trying to suggest you need a PhD to gain a feel for the rightish answer with AI. I'm saying the beginner, unknowingly, incurs the greatest personal cost with AI usage. This is a reminder that you are more capable than you might think. Get curious about the field and find content from trusted human sources. Go to college or read a few books. Anything you can do to increase your understanding of the topic itself will give you a better chance of spotting when AI is making stuff up.

Aim for the intermediate category and become an expert on a few topics.

If you're serious about leveraging AI in any capacity and want to remain safe. AI disconnected education is required and critical.
